## PART A — What We're Building

### 0. Core Architectural Principle

**"The AI is an assistive component, not the clinical authority."**

The AI collects, clarifies, structures, flags, and summarizes. It never independently diagnoses, prescribes, or represents itself as a physician. You remain solely responsible for clinical assessment, diagnosis, treatment decisions, prescription, and referral. Every section below enforces this structurally — through data separation and required physician actions — not just as a policy statement.

### 1. Product Framing

This is not a healthcare website with a chatbot bolted on. It is a **physician-led telemedicine platform** with a public website, a patient portal, a physician clinical workspace, and a secure backend. The public website is the entry point; the digital clinical workflow is the actual product.

End-to-end flow:

```
Public website → Patient portal → Consultation → AI history / voice note
   → Safety screening → Doctor clinical workspace → Video consultation
   → Doctor assessment → Doctor-approved prescription / referral → Follow-up
```

The physician is the clinical authority at every decision point in this chain — nothing here routes a clinical decision through the AI.

### 2. Central Clinical Architecture — The Consultation Spine

**Patient → Consultation → Clinical Record**

As of Phase 4b, "Patient" here means a **family member** — the person a consultation is actually for — which is a separate concept from the **account** (the login). One account can hold several family members (see Section 5a); every consultation still attaches to exactly one family member, so the spine below is unchanged in shape, only in what sits at its root.

Everything belonging to a specific consultation attaches to that consultation. Nothing is designed as a standalone module that would later make this relationship awkward. The full attach-list, expanded per the addendum:

Appointment · patient complaint · patient-reported information · AI-guided history · original voice note · voice transcription · AI-structured history · AI summary · safety/red-flag events · doctor assessment · doctor-confirmed diagnosis · treatment plan · prescription · referral · follow-up · consultation status · payment status · audit events.

Validated against the database chain (still conceptual — schema is Phase 3):

**Patient → Consultation → History → Safety Event → Doctor Assessment → Prescription → Follow-up → Audit Trail**

- One Patient → many Consultations.
- One Consultation → one history record (bundling voice note/transcript/structured Q&A as applicable), one appointment, one payment status, one consultation status.
- One Consultation → zero, one, or many Safety Events, each timestamped and linked to the exact history item that triggered it.
- One Consultation → one Doctor Assessment → optionally one Prescription, one Referral, one Follow-up plan.
- Every record above generates Audit Trail entries.

This remains a clean hierarchy anchored on Consultation. **On multi-doctor readiness:** every Consultation carries a `doctor_id` from day one (even with only you as the doctor, and currently unused — the doctor dashboard that populates it is Phase 7), and every Doctor-owned view will be scoped by that field rather than assumed to be "the one doctor." This is a small modeling decision made now that avoids a structural rebuild later — see Section 20 and the confirmation in Section 22. Implemented in `supabase/migrations/0003_family_members.sql` (Phase 4b).

## PART B — The Three User-Facing Areas

### 3. Overview

The platform has three conceptual areas: the **public website** (marketing/trust/entry point), the **patient portal** (booking, history, dashboard), and the **physician clinical workspace** (the actual clinical tool). All three sit on the same backend and the same Consultation spine — they are views into one system, not separate products.

### 4. Public Website

Purpose: explain the service, establish trust, explain physician-led care, mention that technology assists without overemphasizing it, show the fee, let patients start booking. (Revised per physician feedback, Phase Log — the site leads with the physician, not the AI; "AI" appears only where a visitor would naturally ask about it, e.g. an FAQ entry.)

Pages: Home · How It Works · Services/Common Complaints · About · FAQ · Contact · Privacy · Terms · Login · Book Consultation.

Positioning (Section 19 has more): **"Real physician care, supported by smart technology."** Never "AI doctor" or "AI diagnoses you."

### 5. Patient Portal

Mobile-first, minimal data collection. Two core pages initially:

- **Registration/Login** — only information actually necessary to run a consultation safely (name, contact, basic demographics); no unnecessary personal data collection.
- **Dashboard** — upcoming consultation with a Join button, consultation status, previous consultations, prescriptions, follow-ups, payments, profile.

### 5a. Family Accounts (added Phase 4b)

One login does not have to mean one patient. Registering creates an **account** (the login, via Supabase Auth) and automatically adds the account holder as their own **family member** ("Self"). From the dashboard, the account holder can add other people to the account — spouse, children, parents, siblings, other — without each of them registering separately:

- **Adults** (18+, by date of birth): free to add, gated by a required attestation — a checkbox confirming the person has agreed to let the account holder manage their consultations, or that the account holder holds legal authority to do so. This was an explicit decision (Section 38: change discussed and confirmed before implementing), chosen over a slower invite-and-accept flow because it matches how families actually want to use this — "a father will not feel comfortable making a new portal for each member."
- **Minors:** no attestation required — a parent/guardian adding their own child doesn't need to attest to their own parental authority. Date of birth is what the system uses to tell adults and minors apart, not the relationship label alone (a "Child" could be an adult).
- Every consultation belongs to exactly one family member, chosen when booking (Section 6). The account holder sees and manages all of their family's consultations from one dashboard.
- Not yet built, flagged for later: a grown family member eventually getting their own independent login rather than staying under a parent's account indefinitely.

### 6. Booking & Complaint Categories

Booking now starts with **"who is this for?"** — the account holder picks a family member (or adds one on the spot) before choosing a reason for the visit. The patient then selects a reason for the visit from complaint categories rather than free text alone: Fever, Cough, Sore throat, Abdominal pain, Diarrhea/vomiting, Headache, Back pain, Urinary symptoms, Shortness of breath, Chest pain, Other. This matches the original "complaint-based history, not hundreds of disease-bots" principle. Exact first module(s) to build: your decision (Section 22/17).

### 7. AI Consent (Consultation-Level, Not Just Terms-of-Service)

Accepting the general website terms is **not** sufficient consent for AI-assisted history-taking. Before AI history begins, the patient sees plain-language information: AI helps collect/organize information; AI is not a doctor and doesn't diagnose or prescribe; a physician reviews everything; AI may misunderstand or miss things; a voice note is a valid alternative; emergencies should not be routed through the AI history process.

Consent is captured as a discrete record, not implied: **patient, consultation, consent version, timestamp, exactly what was consented to.** Final wording needs your review and, before real-patient launch, legal/privacy review (Section 24/25).

### 8. Two Methods of Providing History — Genuine Patient Choice

**Option A — AI-guided history.** The AI follows an *approved, complaint-specific* question structure (e.g., "When did your fever start? — Today / 1–2 days ago / 3–5 days ago / More than 5 days / Not sure"). It is not an unrestricted open conversation — it runs inside the Clinical Configuration Layer's approved question set for that complaint (Section 15).

**Option B — Voice note.** The patient records their own account instead. The interface presents both options with equal prominence — the voice-note path is never made harder to find in order to push AI usage. Immediately before recording, the patient is told a free-form voice note may not cover everything a structured history would ask, so the doctor may need to ask follow-up questions live.

### 9. Voice Note Architecture

```
Original patient voice → Audio storage → Transcription → AI structuring → Physician review
```

The original recording is preserved (per the eventual retention policy) and stays available to you alongside the transcript — never replaced by it. You can always distinguish: original recording → transcript → AI-structured information → physician-confirmed information. If transcription quality is poor, you are warned rather than shown a silently degraded transcript.

Uncertainty is preserved, never upgraded: patient says *"I think my fever was around 101"* → recorded as **patient-reported**: "approximately 101°F, patient-estimated" — never as "Temperature = 101°F confirmed."

## PART C — Data Integrity & Safety

### 10. Information Provenance — Four Levels, Never Blended

1. **Patient-reported** — the patient's own words, verbatim, hedging included.
2. **AI-generated/structured** — the AI's organization of patient-reported information; always labeled, always traceable to its source.
3. **Physician-confirmed** — you've explicitly reviewed and accepted it. Never automatic.
4. **Physician-authored** — assessment, diagnosis, treatment plan, prescription, referral, follow-up — typed by you, never generated on your behalf.

Worked example, shown as it would appear in your workspace:

```
Patient reported:  "I've had fever for about three days."
AI structured:     Duration: approximately 3 days
Physician:         [✓ Confirmed]
```

Promotion between levels always requires an explicit physician action; the original AI-generated version stays visible even after confirmation.

### 11. AI Uncertainty — Named States, Not a Confidence Score

No numerical "confidence score" (false precision, easy to over- or under-trust). Instead, explicit flags visible to you: **missing information, unclear answer, contradictory answers, possible transcription error, patient uncertainty, requires physician clarification.** A calibrated numerical measure would only be considered later with a specific technical justification, proposed separately — never adopted by default.

### 12. Emergency / Red-Flag Architecture

The AI does not decide what counts as an emergency.

```
Patient response → Deterministic clinical safety rules → Approved red-flag criteria → Escalation if triggered
```

The AI may help surface or present information; the actual criteria are physician-approved deterministic rules, versioned in the Clinical Configuration Layer (Section 15). The system supports: a patient-facing emergency warning, interruption/escalation of the routine workflow where appropriate, an urgent flag on the doctor dashboard, a Safety Event record (timestamp, rule/version that triggered, patient response, system action, physician action, final status).

Patient-facing wording (draft concept, needs your + legal review before production): *"If you think you are having a medical emergency, do not wait for an online consultation. Seek immediate emergency medical care or contact your local emergency service."*

## PART D — The Physician Clinical Workspace

### 13. Doctor Dashboard — Not a Generic Admin Panel

Framed as a **clinical command center**, not a back-office tool. Fast, information-dense, clinically organized, low-click — never visually complicated just to look advanced. It should let you answer, at a glance: who is this patient, why are they here, what did they tell us, what's missing/uncertain, were any safety flags triggered, what did the patient actually say, what do I need to clarify, what's my assessment, what's my treatment decision, do they need referral/follow-up.

### 14. Doctor Dashboard — Pages

- **Dashboard** — today's consultations, completed/waiting/follow-ups, safety alerts, basic activity/revenue info.
- **Consultation queue** — time, patient, complaint, history-ready status, overall status; safety flags highly visible (⚠ not buried).
- **Clinical workspace** (the most important screen) — one view per active consultation: patient demographics, safety status, chief complaint (patient's own words), AI-structured history, unclear/missing items, voice note (recording + transcript), AI summary (clearly labeled), then your assessment, diagnosis, plan, prescription, referral, and follow-up — without navigating across unrelated pages mid-consultation.
- **Safety panel** — a dedicated section (never folded into the general AI summary): current status, previous events, rule/version that triggered, patient response, system action, doctor action — full traceability.
- **AI history detail** — the literal question/answer exchange, so you can inspect the actual interaction when needed, with AI notes distinguishing patient statement from AI interpretation.
- **Voice note view** — play original recording, transcript, AI-structured information, transcription-quality warnings, with a direct path back to the original audio.

### 15. Prescription, Diagnosis & Referral — Physician-Controlled

Clinical decision-making belongs to you: assessment, diagnosis, treatment plan, prescription, advice, referral, follow-up are all doctor-entered/chosen. A deliberate final action is required to make a prescription real:

```
Save draft → Physician review → Approve & Issue
```

The AI cannot independently issue an actionable prescription — there is no code path from AI-generated content directly to an issued prescription. Every issuance is audit-logged (who, what, when).

### 16. Patient Prescription View & Follow-Up

After approval, the patient sees physician information, date, assessment (where appropriate), prescription, advice, follow-up, referral — with PDF download once implemented — and physician-authored content is visually distinguished from AI-generated assistance. Follow-up records carry a date, reason, status, and a link back to the originating consultation; the doctor dashboard will eventually group these as Today / Tomorrow / Overdue.

**Follow-up fee control (requirement captured 2026-09-10, built 2026-09-12 — see Phase Log):** the physician wants each follow-up consultation's fee handled as a doctor-controlled decision, not a fixed platform rule — some follow-ups should be free, some should carry the normal (or a different) fee, and the doctor decides which per case. Conceptually this needs: (a) a follow-up consultation to carry a link back to the consultation it follows up on (extending the existing Follow-up record above), and (b) a fee-control field on that consultation the doctor sets explicitly — e.g. waived vs. the standard PKR 500 vs. a custom amount — rather than the fee always defaulting the same way. Because every consultation already carries a `doctor_id` (Section 2), this naturally becomes a per-doctor decision without further rework once multiple doctors exist. This is Doctor Dashboard (Phase 7) and Payment (Phase 10) territory, not Phase 6 — recorded here now so it shapes those phases' design rather than being bolted on later, per Section 38 (payment logic and consultation-table relationships are exactly the kind of change that gets discussed before it's implemented, not decided silently). Still open when we get there: whether the doctor sets this per-follow-up manually every time, or sets a default policy (e.g. "follow-ups within N days are free unless I override") that individual follow-ups can still override. **Refund vs. waiver, clarified 2026-09-12:** these are two different things. A fee *waiver* (marking a consultation free, or a custom amount) is just a field the doctor sets and doesn't depend on anything else existing. An actual *refund* requires a real payment gateway to already be collecting real payments (Phase 10) — there is no payment collection at all yet, so there is nothing to refund yet. Confirmed sequencing: build the waiver field once Phase 7's core prescription loop and dashboard summary are done; design refund handling only once Phase 10's gateway is chosen and built, so the two aren't designed apart from each other.

### 17. Clinical Configuration Layer

Clinical content is never hard-coded into application logic — it lives in a separate, versioned layer so any production clinical content traces to who approved it and when:

```
Fever Module — Version 1.0
  History questions
  Red flags
  Referral criteria
  Emergency wording
  Guideline sources
  Approved by: [physician] — Approval date: [date]
```

Holds: history questions per module, red-flag criteria, referral criteria, emergency wording, consent wording, guideline references (source/name/version/date/section), protocol version, approval record. No content is written here without your review and approval — Claude may draft, never finalize, clinical content.

### 18. AI Global Kill Switch

You (or an admin) can disable AI functionality platform-wide — outage, unexpected behavior, safety concern, policy change, cost spike, technical malfunction — without stopping the platform:

```
AI history = OFF          Doctor-led consultation = STILL AVAILABLE
```

Patients fall back to a manual structured form and/or a plain voice note without AI structuring; you run the full consultation, assessment, and prescription workflow unaffected. The platform is never architected to depend on AI availability to function.

## PART E — Payments

### 19. Payment Architecture — External Gateway, Not a Custom Processor

**Confirmed: the platform will integrate an external, established payment gateway. It will not build its own payment processor.**

```
Patient → Platform → External payment gateway → Payment confirmation
   → Secure backend → Consultation marked paid → Appointment confirmed
```

The platform stores only necessary metadata: consultation ID, provider, transaction/reference ID, amount, currency, status, timestamp. **No card credentials are stored.**

### 20. Payment Verification & Settlement

Payment success is never inferred from the browser, a redirect, or a client-side flag — it is confirmed only through the gateway's server-side webhook/callback/API, per that provider's official integration. Only a verified server-side confirmation flips `payment_status = PAID`. Settlement runs through the gateway into your registered merchant/bank account; exact provider, fees, and settlement schedule are decided during the Payment phase (Phase 10) — not now. Payment infrastructure is built after the clinical workflow is proven with synthetic patients, but before any real patient is charged.

### 41. How This Actually Gets Built, and Real Limitations

Practical working model, so every future session (including ones that don't remember this conversation) operates the same way:

- **Where the code lives:** built and tested in Claude's own working environment, then committed to a Git/GitHub repository phase by phase. The repository plus this Project's docs are the only things that persist between sessions — a new session has no memory of past conversations, so anything not written to one of those two places is effectively lost. This is why the blueprint gets updated rather than just discussed.
- **Accounts and money:** Claude cannot create or hold the Supabase, Vercel, domain-registrar, payment-gateway, or video-provider accounts — those must be Zayn's own accounts, since he's the business owner and the one who can accept their terms and receive settlement. Claude prepares exact click-by-click instructions and all integration code; Zayn creates the account and pastes the resulting key/token into the place Claude specifies (never into chat, never into browser-visible code).
- **What Claude can verify directly:** code correctness, local behavior, automated tests, and visual review (screenshots) inside its own environment. What it cannot verify without Zayn's real (test-mode) credentials: actual payment-gateway behavior, a live video-call session, or a real SMS/WhatsApp send — those get tested together once accounts exist, in sandbox/demo mode first.
- **Legal/regulatory compliance is not something Claude can certify.** Claude flags where legal or privacy review is needed (see Section 26) but is not a substitute for a lawyer, particularly for Pakistan's telemedicine and health-data rules before real patients are involved.
- **Security and correctness are engineering practices, not guarantees.** Claude follows established best practices and builds in checkpoints, audits, and tests, but no engineer — human or AI — can promise unhackable or bug-free software. A real security review before real patient data is the standard being held to here, not skipped for cost (Section 31).
- **Claude can make mistakes** — misremembering a library detail, missing an edge case — which is exactly why nothing ships without testing each phase, and why clinical content specifically never goes to production on Claude's judgment alone (Section 0, Section 17).
- **Claude is not "on call."** Approvals, live incident response, and any action requiring a human decision-maker in the moment are Zayn's — Claude prepares the tooling and instructions, not the 2am response.
- **Concrete network limitation, discovered in Phase 3 (2026-09-09):** Claude's working environment sits behind an organization-managed network filter that only allows a specific allowlist of destinations (GitHub, the npm/PyPI/crates package registries, Anthropic's own API) — it is not general internet access, and Claude does not attempt to route around it. Supabase and Vercel are both outside that allowlist, confirmed by direct test. Practical effect: Claude can write and locally build/type-check all code, but cannot make a live call from its own environment to Supabase (or any other not-allowlisted third-party service) to test against Zayn's real project. Two ways around this, used depending on the situation: (a) **Zayn runs it locally** — install Node.js once, then `npm install && npm run dev` in the project folder, using a `.env.local` file with the real project keys, and test in his own browser (this is the default for now); or (b) **deploy to a real public URL** (e.g., Vercel) — once the app is actually deployed on the internet rather than running only on `localhost`, Claude's browser tools (which run on Zayn's own device/network, not Claude's sandboxed one) can reach and test it directly, and this removes the friction for every later phase that needs a live third-party call too (payment gateway, video, transcription). Not set up yet — Claude will suggest it again when the friction of local-only testing outweighs the one-time setup cost, or Zayn can ask for it sooner.

### 42. APIs in the Plan — What's Costed vs. Still Open

Third-party APIs are already part of the architecture, but they split into two groups.

**Already named and factored into planning:** the AI provider API for history-taking (Sections 1/7/32; included in the Stage A estimate, provider swappable by design); the database/auth API, Supabase itself (backbone of the whole system, included in every stage's hosting cost); hosting/deployment via Vercel (included).

**Named in the architecture, but priced only provisionally, pending a provider decision (Section 26):** the transcription API for voice notes (mentioned qualitatively in Stage B as "possibly a paid transcription service," not yet quantified); the payment gateway API (a per-transaction fee, not a flat cost — exact percentage depends on the Phase 10 provider); the video consultation API/SDK (flagged as an open gap in Section 23, typically priced per-minute or per-participant, not yet added to any cost tier); a future notification API for SMS/WhatsApp/email (not MVP, not costed).

**The gap worth naming honestly:** the cost tiers in Section 31 were built around *flat infrastructure* costs — hosting and database plans. They don't yet fully capture *usage-based* API costs — AI tokens, transcription minutes, video minutes, message sends — which scale with how many consultations actually happen, not with a fixed monthly fee. At synthetic/low volume that's a rounding error; at "thousands of regular patients," it likely becomes the largest cost line, possibly larger than hosting itself. A real number isn't possible yet — it depends on which AI, video, and transcription providers we choose and their per-unit pricing — but a concrete per-consultation API cost will be calculated at each relevant phase (5, 9, and the voice-note work) rather than left as a placeholder. Worth keeping in view heading into the pilot: the PKR 500 consultation fee needs to comfortably cover this variable per-visit API cost, not just fixed hosting.
